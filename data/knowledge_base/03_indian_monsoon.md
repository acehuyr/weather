# The Indian Monsoon

## What Drives It

The monsoon is a seasonal reversal of wind direction caused by the difference
in how fast land and sea change temperature. Through spring the Indian
landmass heats far faster than the surrounding ocean, creating a low-pressure
zone over northwest India. Moist air is drawn in from the Indian Ocean towards
that low, and when it is forced upward by terrain and convection it cools,
condenses, and rains.

In winter the gradient reverses: the land cools faster than the sea, and dry
air flows outward from the continent.

## The Southwest Monsoon

The southwest monsoon runs from June to September and delivers roughly 75
percent of India's annual rainfall.

Typical onset progression:

- Around 1 June: Kerala coast
- Around 5 to 10 June: Karnataka, Tamil Nadu, and the northeast
- Around 10 to 11 June: Mumbai and the Konkan coast
- Around 15 June: Gujarat and central India
- End of June to early July: Delhi and northwest India
- By around 8 July: the whole country

Withdrawal begins from northwest India around 17 September and completes over
most of the country by mid-October.

Onset dates vary by a week or more from year to year. An onset that is late by
several weeks, or a long break in the rains during July and August, has direct
consequences for sowing and for reservoir levels.

## The Northeast Monsoon

The northeast or retreating monsoon runs from October to December and is the
main rainfall season for Tamil Nadu, coastal Andhra Pradesh, Rayalaseema, and
parts of Kerala and Karnataka. Winds blow from land to sea but pick up moisture
crossing the Bay of Bengal. For Chennai, rainfall in October and November is
normal and expected, while the same rainfall in July would be unusual. This is
exactly the kind of context that raw numbers alone cannot supply.

## Measuring a Monsoon Season

India Meteorological Department classifies the seasonal total against the Long
Period Average, the mean of seasonal rainfall over a long reference period:

- Normal: 96 to 104 percent of the Long Period Average
- Below normal: 90 to 96 percent
- Deficient: less than 90 percent
- Above normal: 104 to 110 percent
- Excess: more than 110 percent

These bands are applied both nationally and at the level of meteorological
subdivisions, which matters because a nationally normal monsoon routinely
contains both flood-hit and drought-hit subdivisions.

## Monsoon Breaks and Active Spells

The monsoon does not rain continuously. Active spells of several days of heavy
rain alternate with break periods of a week or more with little rain. This is
normal behaviour, not a failure of the monsoon. Analysis of a single dry week
during July should not be presented as a deficit until it is compared against
the typical frequency of break spells.

## Regional Contrasts

Rainfall in India is extremely uneven. The Western Ghats and the northeast
receive very high totals, with Mawsynram and Cherrapunji among the wettest
inhabited places on earth. Western Rajasthan receives very little. Comparing
absolute rainfall across regions is unhelpful; comparing each place against its
own normal is the correct approach.
