# Extreme Weather: Official Criteria and Thresholds

Using published thresholds instead of invented ones is what lets a system say
very heavy rainfall rather than a lot of rain. The criteria below follow India
Meteorological Department practice.

## Rainfall Categories (24-hour totals)

- Trace: less than 0.1 mm
- Very light rain: 0.1 to 2.4 mm
- Light rain: 2.5 to 15.5 mm
- Moderate rain: 15.6 to 64.4 mm
- Heavy rain: 64.5 to 115.5 mm
- Very heavy rain: 115.6 to 204.4 mm
- Extremely heavy rain: 204.5 mm or more

A rainy day is a day recording 2.5 mm or more.

## Heat Wave

A heat wave is declared only when the maximum temperature reaches at least
40 C in the plains, 37 C on the coast, or 30 C in hill stations. Once that
floor is met, the classification uses the departure from the normal maximum
for that date:

- Heat wave: departure of 4.5 to 6.4 C above normal
- Severe heat wave: departure of 6.5 C or more above normal

An alternative absolute rule also applies: a maximum of 45 C or more is a heat
wave, and 47 C or more is a severe heat wave, regardless of departure.

The departure-based definition is the reason a purely threshold-based system
gives wrong answers. 39 C in Srinagar is a far more serious anomaly than 39 C
in Nagpur, even though the second number is higher.

## Cold Wave

For the plains, a cold wave requires the minimum temperature to be 10 C or
below, with a departure below normal of 4.5 to 6.4 C. A departure of 6.5 C or
more is a severe cold wave. As with heat waves, the qualifying floor and the
departure are both required.

## Thunderstorms and Squalls

A squall is a sudden increase in wind speed to at least 40 km/h sustained for
at least one minute. Thunderstorms are reported when thunder is heard,
regardless of rainfall amount. Gust speeds, not mean wind speeds, determine
damage potential.

## Tropical Cyclones

Cyclone intensity over the north Indian Ocean is classified by maximum
sustained surface wind speed:

- Depression: 31 to 49 km/h
- Deep depression: 50 to 61 km/h
- Cyclonic storm: 62 to 88 km/h
- Severe cyclonic storm: 89 to 117 km/h
- Very severe cyclonic storm: 118 to 165 km/h
- Extremely severe cyclonic storm: 166 to 221 km/h
- Super cyclonic storm: 222 km/h or more

The two cyclone seasons for the region are April to June and October to
December. The Bay of Bengal produces more and stronger systems than the Arabian
Sea.

## Colour-Coded Warnings

India Meteorological Department issues impact-based warnings on a four-colour
scale:

- Green: no action required, no adverse weather expected
- Yellow: be aware, watch the situation
- Orange: be prepared, disruption is likely
- Red: take action, severe impact is expected

The colour depends on both severity and expected impact, which is why a red
warning in a dense city can be issued at a lower rainfall total than an orange
warning in a sparsely populated area.

## Urban Flooding

Urban flooding depends on rainfall intensity in mm per hour rather than on the
daily total, because drainage systems are designed to a peak-flow capacity.
Intensity above roughly 25 to 30 mm per hour overwhelms most Indian urban
drainage. A 100 mm day spread evenly causes little disruption; 100 mm in three
hours can halt a city. When an analysis reports only daily totals, that
limitation should be stated explicitly.
